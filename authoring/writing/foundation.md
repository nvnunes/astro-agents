# Foundation

## Purpose
Use this style for shareable foundation documents such as concept notes, framework overviews, design charters, and guiding documents. These documents define structure, intent, scope, and expected outputs rather than report scientific results. Inherit the common documentation-writing discipline from `authoring/writing/base.md`, then apply the additional rules below.

## Success Criteria
- Preserve the role of the document as a conceptual or guiding document.
- Improve clarity, precision, and flow.
- Reduce unnecessary abstraction, repetition, and rhetorical framing.
- Keep the revised text usable as a stable foundation for later implementation documents.

## Style
- Use a professional academic tone.
- Keep the prose clear, controlled, and serious.
- Prefer short, simple sentences.
- Allow forward-looking and normative language where the document is defining aims, structure, or design intent.

## Language Discipline
- Prefer direct phrasing over inflated or institutional language.
- Allow abstract terms when the document is defining the abstract structure itself, but anchor them in concrete outputs, records, decisions, or workflows.
- Minimize adjectives and qualifiers. Keep them only if they change meaning.
- Avoid vague praise words such as `powerful`, `important`, `exciting`, or `transformative`.
- Avoid conversational, rhetorical, or promotional phrasing.
- Let each sentence carry one main idea.
- Remove filler, repetition, and empty framing.

## Explanatory Balance
- Define concepts clearly enough that later implementation documents can follow from them without guesswork.
- Keep motivation brief and subordinate to structure.
- Explain relationships between parts directly.
- Use concrete examples of outputs or records when they improve clarity.

## Terminology
- Define key terms before relying on them.
- Use terminology consistently across related documents.
- Keep definitions short and stable.
- Prefer direct verbs such as `defines`, `supports`, `records`, `tracks`, `uses`, `guides`, `documents`, `assesses`, and `structures`.
- Avoid repeated formulations such as `is concerned with` when a more direct verb will do.

## Preservation And Revision
When revising existing text:
- Preserve the role of the document as a conceptual or guiding document.
- Do not change defined terms, scope names, framework names, document roles, or other structural terminology unless instructed or clearly required to fix an error.
- Do not remove necessary normative, conceptual, or forward-looking language if it is part of the document's purpose.
- Do not add claims about implementation status, evidence, consensus, or external validation that are not supported by the source text.
- Improve clarity, concision, and flow.
- Reduce unnecessary abstraction, repetition, and rhetorical framing.
- Do not force foundation documents into paper style when the document type requires conceptual, normative, or forward-looking language.

## Adaptation
Adjust emphasis to the document subtype without changing the overall tone:
- Concept note: allow slightly more motivation where it helps explain why the framework exists or why its structure matters.
- Framework overview: prioritize stable definitions, structure, terminology, and relationships between parts.
- Method or framework document: prioritize steps, decision rules, level meanings, and traceable outputs while keeping the prose shareable and conceptually clear.
- Design charter or guiding document: prioritize constraints, roles, expected outputs, and implementation consequences.
- If subtype is unclear, infer it from the surrounding material. If it remains unclear, default to framework-overview style.

## Output
- When writing or revising a foundation document, return the revised text directly unless explanation is requested.
- When reviewing a foundation document, identify role drift, weak structure, weak terminology control, or weak adaptation before proposing replacement text.
