# Science

## Purpose
Use this style for scientific research writing intended for direct inclusion in theses, journal papers, conference proceedings, and research proposals. Inherit the common writing discipline from `skills/technical-writing/references/base.md`, then apply the stricter scientific constraints below.

## Success Criteria
- Preserve scientific meaning.
- Reduce unnecessary words, abstraction, and repetition.
- Keep the revised text ready for direct inclusion in the target document.

## Style
- Use a clean, professional academic tone.
- Prioritize clarity, precision, and information density.
- Avoid informal, conversational, rhetorical, or expressive language.
- Write for an expert but non-specialist audience.

## Language Discipline
- Prefer direct, concrete phrasing over abstract or conceptual language.
- Use adjectives and qualifiers sparingly. Keep them when they clarify supported novelty, scale, limitation, comparison, uncertainty, or interpretive weight; remove them when they are vague, decorative, or unsupported.
- Avoid vague terms unless they are specified or quantified (e.g., “significant,” “important,” “broad,” “various”).
- Avoid abstract or institutional-sounding nouns unless they are necessary and clearly defined (e.g., “capability,” “functionality,” “operability,” “viability”). Replace them with what is actually measured, computed, selected, or compared.
- Use the simplest correct terminology. Prefer simpler terms over jargon unless the jargon is necessary and defined.
- Make specific claims. Do not write “traditional approaches” or “improves performance” without saying what approach or what improvement.
- Do not bury the main contribution in neutral exposition. When the source supports novelty, contrast, or interpretive importance, state it plainly and specifically.
- Give examples or citations instead of vague references to prior work.
- Do not restate the same point in multiple ways.

## Explanatory Balance
- Provide enough explanation for clarity and self-containment, especially for methods, assumptions, and less-standard choices.
- Do not explain standard concepts unless they are directly relevant.
- Be concise, but do not omit information needed to understand the argument.

## Terminology
- Define key terms, variables, and metrics at first use within a section or document.
- Use terminology consistently.
- Avoid ambiguous or overloaded terms (e.g., “resolution,” “performance,” “coverage,” “efficiency”) unless the intended meaning is clear from context or explicitly defined.
- Do not introduce technical terms if simpler language conveys the same meaning.

## Preservation And Revision
When revising existing text:
- Preserve technical meaning unless instructed otherwise.
- Do not change equations, symbols, variable names, citation keys, labels, numerical values, or stated uncertainty unless instructed or clearly required to fix an error.
- Do not change LaTeX macros, citation commands, cross-references, notation conventions, or document structure unless instructed or clearly required to fix an error.
- Do not add claims, references, interpretation, or implications not supported by the source text.
- Remove unnecessary adjectives, abstraction, repetition, and filler.
- Replace vague language with specific, concrete language.
- Do not introduce stylistic drift.

## Adaptation
Adjust depth and emphasis to the document type without changing the overall tone:
- Thesis: allow slightly more explanation where it improves completeness.
- Paper or proceedings: prioritize density, concision, and directness.
- Proposal: emphasize motivation, justification, and clarity.
- If document type is unclear, infer it from the surrounding material. If it remains unclear, default to paper or proceedings style.

## Output
- When writing or revising scientific text, return the revised text directly unless explanation is requested.
- When reviewing scientific text, identify meaning drift, weak terminology control, weak scientific specificity, or weak output definition before proposing replacement text.
